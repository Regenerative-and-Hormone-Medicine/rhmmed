<h2>The Symptoms Felt Real. The Explanation Never Fully Did.</h2><p>You wake at 3 a.m. again. Too warm, wide awake, with nothing on your mind except the fact that you're awake.</p><p>The next afternoon, a familiar word slips away mid-sentence. Your patience runs out faster than it used to. Sex feels different, or you've quietly stopped wanting it. You may still be functioning on the outside while feeling unlike yourself on the inside.</p><p>Chances are you've already tried to get answers. You brought it up at a checkup, maybe more than once, and heard that your labs look fine, that it's stress, or that it's just part of getting older. Fine doesn't match how you feel. You know your body, and you know something has changed.</p><p>So the question you keep circling back to is a fair one: is this hormones, and could hormone therapy help?</p><p>The clearest signs you may need hormone replacement therapy are hot flashes or night sweats that disrupt your days or sleep, sleep that keeps breaking apart, mood changes or brain fog that feel new, vaginal dryness or painful sex, and changing cycles in your 40s. They matter most when several show up together, last longer than a few months, and you're under 60 or within 10 years of your last period.</p><p>But a list of signs is only half the answer. Many of them can also come from your thyroid, your blood sugar, inflammation, low iron or sleep that never recovers, and the only way to know which is to look.</p><p>If you're a man researching hormone therapy, our <a href="/men/hormone-therapy">hormone therapy for men</a> page explains how we evaluate testosterone and the systems around it.</p><h2>We're Not Here to Convince You That You Need HRT</h2><p>We're here to figure out why you don't feel like yourself anymore.</p><p>Sometimes the answer is hormones. Often it's hormones plus something else. And sometimes it's something else entirely: a thyroid that's struggling, insulin that has been creeping up for years, inflammation, nutrient gaps like low iron or vitamin D, or sleep that never really came back. Hormone therapy can make a meaningful difference for the right woman. Starting it without asking those questions first can mean treating one piece and missing the rest.</p><p>That's what sets RHM apart. We start with why you feel this way. The treatment plan comes after.</p><h2>Why This Keeps Getting Missed</h2><p>This usually comes down to how care is set up: each symptom gets treated as its own problem.</p><p>Brain fog is one problem. Weight gain is another. Anxiety is another. Low libido is another. Poor sleep is another. Each gets its own short visit and its own fix, and nobody steps back to ask the obvious question: what if they're connected?</p><p>For many women in their 40s and 50s, they are. Falling estrogen and progesterone touch your sleep, mood, metabolism and brain at the same time, and your thyroid, blood sugar and inflammation can amplify all of it. Seen one at a time, each symptom looks minor. Seen together, they often tell a clear story.</p><h3>"Your Labs Look Normal." Which Labs?</h3><p>If you've been told your labs are normal, it's worth asking what was actually tested. A routine checkup panel usually covers a small set of markers, and hormones often aren't included at all. Thyroid is commonly screened with a single value, TSH. And every result is judged against a reference range built from a broad population, so "in range" isn't always the same as optimal for you.</p><p>Normal is an average. You are not an average.</p><p>That's why RHM evaluates 160+ biomarkers across hormones, thyroid, insulin and blood sugar regulation, iron, inflammation and more, and reads them as one system alongside your symptoms. We aren't looking at one hormone value and sending you on your way. We're trying to understand what is actually happening in your body.</p><h2>The Signs at a Glance</h2><p>These are the changes that most often bring women to the HRT conversation. One on its own may not mean much. Several together, lasting months, deserve a closer look, and because a few of them overlap with thyroid, metabolic and sleep problems, the pattern matters more than any single sign.</p><ul><li>Hot flashes or night sweats that interrupt your day or your sleep</li><li>Sleep that falls apart, often waking between 2 and 4 a.m.</li><li>Mood changes, anxiety or irritability that feel new</li><li>Brain fog, forgetfulness, or losing words mid-sentence</li><li>Vaginal dryness, discomfort during sex, or more frequent UTIs</li><li>A drop in libido</li><li>Joint aches and stiffness without an obvious cause</li><li>Periods that change in your 40s: shorter, longer, heavier or skipped</li><li>Weight shifting toward your midsection</li><li>Menopause that arrived early, or after surgery</li></ul><h2>Do I Need HRT? Take the 2-Minute Quiz</h2><p>Nine questions about how you've been feeling. Your answers show how your experience lines up with the signs physicians often look for. Think of it as a starting point: it can show whether hormones may be part of the picture, and an evaluation is how you find out what else might be involved.</p><p>PS. This self-check is for educational purposes and is by no means a diagnosis. Only a physician who knows your history can tell you whether hormone therapy is right for you.</p><div data-rt-embed-type='true'><div class="tldr-wrap rhm-quiz" data-quiz="hrt">

Sleep apnea also becomes more common after menopause, so it's worth ruling out if you snore or wake up unrefreshed.</p><h3>Mood changes that feel new</h3><p>Irritability that flares out of proportion. Anxiety you never used to have. A flatness where your usual enthusiasm used to be.</p><p>Estrogen influences serotonin and other mood-regulating chemistry, and the swings of perimenopause can make your emotional baseline feel like it keeps moving. This is physiology, not personal failure. Hormone shifts can also bring depression to the surface, and that deserves its own care, alongside or instead of hormones. Thyroid changes can shift mood too.</p><h3>Brain fog and losing words</h3><p>You walk into a room and forget why. You reach for a familiar word and find nothing there. Many women describe it as thinking through a haze.</p><p>Brain fog is common during the transition, and for many women it eases afterward. We cover what helps in our guide to <a href="/articles/perimenopause-brain-fog">perimenopause brain fog</a>.</p><p>What else it can be: thyroid changes, low iron or B12, and months of broken sleep can all cloud your thinking. That overlap is exactly why we test before we treat.</p><figure class="w-richtext-figure-type-image w-richtext-align-fullwidth" style="max-width:1448px" data-rt-type="image" data-rt-align="fullwidth" data-rt-max-width="1448px"><div><img alt="" src="/images/cms/6abe63aa74d221890ee9ef85_brain_fog.png" loading="lazy"></div></figure><h3>Vaginal dryness, painful sex or recurring UTIs</h3><p>Lower estrogen thins and dries the tissue of the vagina and urethra. That can show up as dryness, burning, discomfort during sex, more urgency, or urinary infections that keep coming back. Clinically, this is called genitourinary syndrome of menopause.</p><p>Unlike hot flashes, these changes often don't fade with time. They're also very treatable, and low-dose vaginal estrogen is an option physicians often consider separately from systemic HRT. You can read more about it in our <a href="/women/sexual-health">female sexual health</a> care.</p><h3>A drop in libido</h3><p>Desire that has gone quiet, or arousal that takes longer than it used to. Estrogen, progesterone and testosterone all play a part, and so do sleep, mood and how comfortable sex physically feels.</p><p>It's one of the most common reasons women first raise hormones with a physician. It counts. Thyroid function, iron and chronic stress can play a part too.</p><h3>Joint aches and stiffness</h3><p>Stiff hands in the morning. Knees and shoulders that ache without an injury. Estrogen has anti-inflammatory effects in joint tissue, and many women notice new aches around menopause.</p><p>Joint pain has plenty of other causes too, so it works best as a supporting sign alongside the others on this list.</p><h3>Periods that change in your 40s</h3><p>Cycles that get shorter, longer or heavier, or skip months entirely, are often the first sign of <a href="/women/perimenopause">perimenopause</a>, sometimes years before your last period. Hot flashes and sleep changes can begin here too, while your cycle is still going.</p><p>You don't have to wait for your periods to stop before talking about treatment.</p><h3>Weight shifting to your midsection</h3><p>Same habits, different shape. As estrogen falls, fat tends to move from the hips and thighs toward the abdomen, and insulin sensitivity often changes along with it.</p><p>Hormone therapy is not a weight-loss treatment, but this shift belongs to the same picture. We explain it in depth in <a href="/articles/perimenopause-weight-gain">perimenopause weight gain</a>.</p><p>What else it can be: insulin resistance can build for years while fasting glucose still looks normal. Our guide to the <a href="/articles/insulin-resistance-test">insulin resistance test</a> explains what to ask for.</p><figure class="w-richtext-figure-type-image w-richtext-align-fullwidth" style="max-width:1448px" data-rt-type="image" data-rt-align="fullwidth" data-rt-max-width="1448px"><div><img alt="" src="/images/cms/6abe64a61fca9fe4a091109e_weight_shifting.png" loading="lazy"></div></figure><h3>Early or surgical menopause</h3><p>If your periods stopped before 40, or before 45, or your ovaries were surgically removed, your body loses estrogen years earlier than average. This is one of the clearest reasons to talk about hormone therapy, even without severe symptoms.</p><p>For women with early menopause, <a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35797481/" target="_blank">The Menopause Society's guidance</a> generally recommends hormone therapy until around the average age of natural menopause, unless there's a medical reason not to.</p><h2>When Should You Start HRT? The Timing Window</h2><p>When you start matters almost as much as whether you start.</p><p>The Menopause Society's <a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35797481/" target="_blank">2022 position statement on hormone therapy</a> concludes that for women younger than 60, or within 10 years of their last period, who have no reason to avoid it, the benefits of hormone therapy outweigh the risks for treating bothersome symptoms. Starting later than that, the balance becomes less favorable, because the risks of heart disease, stroke and blood clots rise with age and time since <a href="/women/menopause">menopause</a>.</p><p>Less favorable is a different thing from no. Symptoms don't follow a calendar: <a href="https://menopause.org/press-releases/hormone-therapy-usage-rates-still-low-despite-proven-benefits" target="_blank">up to 40% of women in their 60s still have hot flashes</a>. For women further into <a href="/women/postmenopause">postmenopause</a>, the decision becomes more individual. The type of hormone, the route (a patch or gel rather than a pill, for example), the dose and your personal risk factors all carry more weight. That's a conversation for a physician who knows your full history.</p><p>And if you're in your 40s with changing cycles and real symptoms, you're not too early. Perimenopause is often when symptoms are at their most disruptive.</p><h2>When HRT Isn't the Answer</h2><p>Not every woman needs HRT, and we'd rather tell you that than prescribe it by default. The right plan depends on what's driving your symptoms and on your health history.</p><h3>When systemic HRT is usually avoided</h3><p>Physicians are generally cautious about, or avoid, systemic hormone therapy if you have a history of:</p><ul><li>Breast cancer or another hormone-sensitive cancer</li><li>A blood clot in the leg or lung</li><li>A stroke or heart attack</li><li>Active liver disease</li><li>Vaginal bleeding that hasn't been explained</li></ul><p>If any of these apply to you, it doesn't mean you have to live with your symptoms. Non-hormonal treatments exist for hot flashes, sleep and mood, and local options may still be possible for vaginal symptoms. It means your plan starts with your history.</p><h3>When the symptoms point somewhere else</h3><p>Several conditions can look a lot like menopause:</p><ul><li><strong>Thyroid dysfunction</strong> can cause fatigue, weight change, mood shifts, heat intolerance and brain fog. Our <a href="/women/thyroid">thyroid</a> care looks at this in depth.</li><li><strong>Iron deficiency</strong>, which is common with heavy perimenopausal periods, can drain energy and focus.</li><li><strong>Sleep apnea</strong> breaks up sleep and can leave you foggy and irritable.</li><li><strong>Depression or anxiety</strong> can overlap with hormonal mood changes and deserves direct care.</li><li><strong>Medications</strong>, including some antidepressants and blood pressure drugs, can cause flushing or sleep changes.</li></ul><p>For most women over 45, menopause itself is recognized from symptoms and cycle changes rather than a single blood test, because hormone levels swing from day to day during the transition. Where testing earns its keep is in ruling out what looks like hormones and isn't, and in seeing the whole picture before treatment begins.</p><h2>Isn't HRT Dangerous? What Changed Since 2002</h2><p>If you hesitate at the word "hormones," you're far from alone. In 2002, early results from the Women's Health Initiative linked hormone therapy to higher rates of breast cancer, heart disease and stroke. Prescriptions dropped almost overnight, and a generation of women was told to tough it out.</p><p>What got lost was nuance. The women in that study were, on average, in their 60s and often well past menopause, and they took one specific oral combination. Long-term follow-up tells a fuller story. <a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28898378/" target="_blank">After 18 years, women who took hormone therapy had no higher overall mortality</a> than women who took a placebo: 27.1% compared with 27.6%.</p><p>Hormone therapy still carries real risks for some women, and those risks depend on your age, your history, the hormones used and how they're delivered. That's why the decision belongs to a careful evaluation, not a headline. We go deeper on what changed in our guide to <a href="/women/hormone-replacement-therapy">hormone replacement therapy for women</a>.</p><h2>How RHM Does Things Differently</h2><p>We start by listening. Could it still be hormones?</h3><p>It can. Hormone levels swing from day to day during perimenopause, so one normal result doesn't rule it out, and a routine panel may not check hormones, a full thyroid picture or insulin at all. It's worth asking which labs were run and reading them alongside your symptoms.</p><h3>At what age should a woman start HRT?</h3><p>There's no single right age. Many women start in their 40s or early 50s, when perimenopause or menopause symptoms begin to disrupt their lives. The evidence for benefit is strongest when hormone therapy begins before 60 or within 10 years of your last period. After that, it becomes an individualized decision.</p><h3>Do I need HRT if I don't have symptoms?</h3><p>Usually not. For most women, HRT is used to treat symptoms that affect quality of life. The main exception is early or surgical menopause, where hormone therapy is often recommended until around the average age of menopause to support long-term bone and heart health, unless there's a reason to avoid it.</p><h3>Is it too late to start HRT after 60?</h3><p>Not automatically. Risks rise with age and time since menopause, so the decision depends more on your health history, the type of hormone and how it's delivered. Some women in their 60s still benefit, often with non-oral options and careful monitoring.</p><h3>Do you gain weight when you start HRT?</h3><p>Hormone therapy isn't known to cause weight gain on its own. Some women notice bloating or breast tenderness at first, which often settles. Weight changes around menopause are driven by shifting hormones, insulin sensitivity, sleep and muscle mass, and a physician can help you look at all of them together.</p><h3>Can I check my estrogen levels at home?</h3><p>At-home tests exist, but during perimenopause estrogen can swing widely from day to day, so a single reading rarely answers whether you need HRT. Your symptoms, cycle history and a broader lab panel, read together, tell you far more.</p><h3>Do I need HRT after a hysterectomy?</h3><p>It depends on whether your ovaries were removed. If they were, menopause begins right away, and hormone therapy is often discussed, especially if it happens before the average age of menopause. If your ovaries were kept, they usually continue producing hormones, though menopause may arrive somewhat earlier.</p><h3>Could it be my thyroid instead?</h3><p>It could, and it's worth checking. Thyroid problems share many symptoms with menopause, including fatigue, mood changes, brain fog and weight change, and the two can overlap. A full thyroid panel alongside your hormones helps tell them apart.</p><h2>Find Out Why You Don't Feel Like Yourself</h2><p>We're not here to convince you that you need HRT. We're here to figure out why you don't feel like yourself anymore, and what to do about it. Sometimes that's hormone therapy. Sometimes it's your thyroid, your metabolism, inflammation or sleep. Often it's a combination, and the plan is built around what we find.</p><p>If you're ready to stop guessing, understand what may be driving these changes and take back control of your health, we're ready to help.
