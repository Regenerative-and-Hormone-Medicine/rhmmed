<p>You probably didn't start here. You started with being wiped out by mid-afternoon, workouts that stopped paying off, a drive that went missing and a waistline that ignores everything you do. Maybe you asked about it and heard that your labs were fine, that it's stress, or that this is what your forties feel like. So now you're pricing TRT on your own, and the numbers make no sense: $30 a month here, $149 there, a few hundred somewhere else.</p><p>Online TRT prices vary dramatically, but those numbers rarely tell the whole story. Some advertised prices focus primarily on medication, while others reflect a more comprehensive approach to care. The real question isn't simply what testosterone costs, but what's included in your treatment and whether your provider is investigating why you're experiencing symptoms in the first place.</p><p>In a study of more than 10 million insured men, approximately one in four new testosterone users had not had their testosterone measured in the year before starting treatment, according to <a href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4100547/" target="_blank">research published in JAMA Internal Medicine</a>.</p><h2>We're Not Here to Sell You TRT</h2><p>Most pages about TRT cost are written by clinics that sell TRT, so the question they answer is how cheaply you can start. That's a fair question, but it skips the more important one.</p><p><strong>We're not here to convince you that you need TRT. We're here to figure out why you don't feel like yourself anymore.</strong></p><p>Sometimes that answer is testosterone. Often testosterone is part of it, alongside thyroid function, insulin and metabolic health, inflammation, sleep, stress or nutrient status such as iron and vitamin D. Those systems talk to each other, and the symptoms that sent you here can come from any of them. Finding out which one is driving things is what keeps you from paying every month for the wrong fix.</p><h2>Why This Keeps Getting Missed</h2><p>You've likely been handed a separate answer for each symptom. Tired? Another coffee. Weight creeping up? Eat less, train harder. Low drive? Here's a prescription. Short fuse and poor focus? That's stress.</p><p>What if they're connected? Fatigue, weight gain around the middle, low libido, poor sleep and brain fog often travel together because they share the same underlying physiology. A busy primary-care visit is built to rule out disease, so the panel is short. A five-minute online intake is built to approve a prescription. Neither is set up to look at the whole picture.</p><p>And if you've been told "your labs look normal," the useful next question is: which labs? A routine panel often leaves testosterone out entirely. When it is checked, it may be one number, drawn at whatever time of day you made it in, read against a range built from population averages. Being in range is a starting point for the conversation.</p><p>That's why RHM reads hormones, thyroid, metabolic health, inflammation and nutrient status together as one picture, with up to 160+ biomarkers when that picture calls for a deeper look.</p><figure class="w-richtext-figure-type-image w-richtext-align-fullwidth" style="max-width:1536px" data-rt-type="image" data-rt-align="fullwidth" data-rt-max-width="1536px"><div><img alt="Physician discussing testosterone therapy options with a male patient, with medication on the table" src="/images/cms/6ad22a28318c5013a3e6bc5e_Razgovor-o-izboru-testosteronske-terapije.png" loading="lazy"></div></figure><h2>Your Testosterone Number Tells You What. It Doesn't Tell You Why.</h2><p>If you've already seen a low result, it can feel like the case is closed. A number is where the investigation starts, though. Testosterone sits at the end of a chain, and where that chain is weak changes what helps.</p><p><strong>The testosterone chain:</strong> your brain signals for it (the pituitary releases LH) → your testes produce it → most of it travels bound to proteins such as SHBG, with a smaller free share your tissues can use → sleep, body fat, insulin resistance, stress and some medications pull on every link → you feel the result.</p><p>Three places where the chain commonly breaks:</p><ul><li><strong>Signaling from the brain.</strong> The <a href="https://www.endocrine.org/clinical-practice-guidelines/testosterone-therapy" target="_blank">Endocrine Society's guideline</a> recommends measuring LH and FSH to tell testicular causes apart from pituitary or hypothalamic ones. That distinction shapes which treatments make sense, especially if you want children.</li><li><strong>Sleep.</strong> In a small study of young, healthy men, one week of sleeping five hours a night lowered daytime testosterone by 10–15%, against about 1–2% a year from normal aging, per <a href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4445839/" target="_blank">the researchers' report in JAMA</a>. Untreated severe sleep apnea is also on the guideline's list of reasons to hold off on testosterone.</li><li><strong>Body fat and insulin resistance.</strong> In a meta-analysis of weight-loss studies, losing weight through diet or surgery significantly raised total and free testosterone, and the amount lost was the best predictor of the rise, as <a href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5944503/" target="_blank">summarized in this review</a>. <a href="/men/metabolic-health">Our men's metabolic health program</a> looks at insulin directly.</li></ul><p><strong>What we see in our own patients:</strong> these aren't edge cases. Our medical team estimates that well over 75% of the patients who come to RHM have sleep apnea or other sleep problems, and more than half have metabolic or weight concerns. Most still end up on TRT, as an injection, cream or oral medication, because for them the core issue is low testosterone driven by aging and factors such as stress. Your age also shapes how we start treatment and which medication we start with.</p><p>None of this means a low number is wrong or that TRT is a bad choice. For many men with confirmed low testosterone and symptoms, it's the most direct path to feeling like themselves again. The number deserves an explanation before you commit to paying for treatment month after month. If your energy or weight seems tied to blood sugar, <a href="/articles/insulin-resistance-test">this guide to insulin resistance testing</a> explains which markers matter. This is physiology, not personal failure, and it can be measured.</p><p>Getting a testosterone prescription may be easy. Understanding why your testosterone is low requires a more thorough evaluation. Are you paying for answers, or just medication?</p><h2>What You're Actually Paying For</h2><p>You've been comparing monthly prices because that's what every clinic leads with. Underneath, the cost of TRT breaks into the same few line items wherever you go.</p><ul><li><strong>Diagnostic labs</strong> confirm low testosterone on a repeat morning test and point to the cause. A thorough panel covers total and free testosterone, SHBG, LH and FSH, estradiol, a blood count and PSA, plus thyroid and metabolic markers.</li><li><strong>A physician evaluation</strong> decides whether TRT fits you at all, and which form. It covers your history, symptoms, sleep, medications, goals and fertility plans.</li><li><strong>Medication and supplies</strong> can be the smallest line or the biggest one, depending on the form.</li><li><strong>Follow-up labs and visits</strong> catch side effects early and adjust the plan. They track testosterone, blood count, PSA and blood pressure, reviewed with a physician.</li></ul><p>When a clinic charges $150 or $250 a month for generic injections, you're mostly paying for testing, the physician and monitoring. Those are the parts worth comparing.</p><h2>How Much Does TRT Cost by Type?</h2><p>You may have heard that one form is "the cheap one" and another is "the good one." The right form depends on your labs, your routine and how your body responds. Here's what the medication alone typically costs before insurance:</p><ul><li><strong>Generic injections</strong> (cypionate or enanthate) cost about $22–49 per vial with a discount coupon, or $98–186 at retail. One vial often lasts several weeks.</li><li><strong>Brand-name gels and creams</strong> run about $400–1,000 a month.</li><li><strong>Pellets</strong> cost about $1,300 per insertion, plus the in-office procedure, repeated every few months.</li><li><strong>Brand-name oral capsules</strong> run about $1,000–2,000 a month.</li></ul><p>Prices vary by pharmacy, dose and region. If you're still deciding between testosterone and other approaches, our comparison of <a href="/articles/peptide-therapy-vs-trt">peptide therapy vs TRT</a> walks through how the two work.</p><h2>Is Cheap Testosterone Always Safe?</h2><p>Not all testosterone products are held to the same regulatory standards.</p><p>FDA-approved generic testosterone can be both affordable and effective. But compounded, unapproved, or counterfeit products may carry additional risks, including inconsistent dosing or contamination.</p><p>And even when the medication itself is legitimate, the quality of your care matters. Is your treatment being individually evaluated by a qualified medical provider, or are you moving through a standardized process designed for thousands of patients?</p><p>Before choosing a TRT provider based on price, ask yourself: Who is prescribing my medication? Where is it coming from? Is it FDA-approved or compounded, and what quality standards apply to it?</p><p>Because saving money on treatment shouldn't mean compromising on medication quality or clinical oversight.</p><h2>Monthly Price vs First-Year Cost</h2><p>If a low monthly price is what caught your eye, that's understandable. It's also where most surprises hide. Year one nearly always costs more than the years after it, because it includes the diagnostic work, closer labs in the first months, and the adjustments that come with finding your plan.</p><p>That $49 monthly subscription might sound appealing. But does it include your initial evaluation, lab work, follow-up appointments, medication and ongoing monitoring? Or will those costs appear later? If it comes out cheaper by skipping labs, the saving is monitoring you didn't get.</p><p>At RHM, labs, provider visits, medication and shipping are built into the $299, so year one costs the same as every year after it: $3,588, with nothing added later for follow-up labs or appointments.</p><h2>Is TRT Covered by Insurance?</h2><p>You may have been told insurance never covers TRT, or that it covers everything. Usually it's somewhere in between.</p><ul><li><strong>What insurers typically want:</strong> documented low testosterone, usually on more than one morning test, plus symptoms. Prior authorization is common, and many plans favor generic injections.</li><li><strong>Age-related decline alone is often harder to get covered</strong>, since FDA labeling keeps a limitation of use for low testosterone due to aging alone.</li><li><strong>Many specialty clinics are cash-pay.</strong> Your insurance may still cover labs, or reimburse part of a visit if the clinic provides the paperwork.</li><li><strong>HSA and FSA funds</strong> can often be used, because prescribed medicines and medical care count as medical expenses under <a href="https://www.irs.gov/publications/p502" target="_blank">IRS Publication 502</a>. Check your plan's rules first.</li></ul><h2>When TRT Isn't the Right Call (and Why We'll Tell You)</h2><p>If you've been told you're "too young" for testosterone, or that you should just push through, it's frustrating. The flip side is that some clinics say yes to almost everyone. We'd rather tell you when TRT isn't the right fit than prescribe it by default.</p><ul><li><strong>If you want children soon.</strong> Testosterone therapy can lower your own sperm production, and the Endocrine Society recommends against it for men planning fertility in the near term. If having children matters to you, that conversation should happen before your first prescription. Other approaches exist, and <a href="/men/peptide-therapy">peptide-based options</a> are part of that conversation for some men.</li><li><strong>If an underlying condition is contributing.</strong> Untreated sleep apnea, conditions affecting red blood cell production, or pituitary dysfunction may influence hormone health and require a different approach to evaluation and treatment.</li><li><strong>If the monitoring would be skipped.</strong> Testosterone can raise red blood cell counts and blood pressure, and in February 2025 the <a href="https://www.fda.gov/drugs/drug-alerts-and-statements/fda-issues-class-wide-labeling-changes-testosterone-products" target="_blank">FDA required a new blood-pressure warning</a> across testosterone products. The same update reflected the TRAVERSE trial, which found no increase in adverse cardiovascular outcomes in men treated for low testosterone. TRT is well studied, and monitoring is part of doing it well. Who is monitoring those changes, and what happens if something needs attention?</li></ul><p><strong><em>Dr. Rand Insight:</em></strong></p><div data-rt-embed-type='true'><blockquote class='blockquote'><p><em>“Men usually ask me what TRT costs. The first thing I want to know is what's pulling their testosterone down. If it's sleep or weight, treating that may bring it back up. If testosterone itself is the problem, we treat it and keep a close eye on the labs. The prescription is the inexpensive part. Getting the reason right is what saves you money.”</em></p><div class='blockquote_profile'><img src='/images/cms/6a362af7235f0819a188a49f_dr_rand.jpg' loading='lazy' alt='Profile Picture of Dr. Rand' class='profile_img'><p class='body-m fw-500 color-dark'>Dr. Rand McClain</p></div></blockquote></div><h2>Why Testosterone Alone Isn't Always the Whole Treatment Plan</h2><p>When testosterone levels increase during TRT, some of that testosterone naturally converts into estradiol. For certain men, this can lead to elevated estrogen levels that require additional management.</p><p>That's why a comprehensive TRT program should consider more than testosterone levels alone.</p><p>In some cases, medications such as anastrozole may be appropriate to help manage elevated estradiol. In others, adjusting the testosterone dose or treatment schedule may be sufficient. Before you start, ask how your provider handles rising estrogen. At RHM, anastrozole is included in the men's plan when you need it.</p><h2>How RHM Does Things Differently</h2><p>Our <a href="/men/hormone-therapy">physician-led hormone therapy program for men</a> works back along the chain to the cause. The plan might be TRT, treating what's pulling testosterone down, or both. Built around you. Not a template.</p><h3>What the $299 a month includes</h3><ul><li><strong>Medication:</strong> injectable testosterone, plus anastrozole when your estrogen needs managing.</li><li><strong>Three lab panels a year:</strong> at the start, 90 days later, and six months after that.</li><li><strong>Three visits with a licensed medical provider</strong>, on the same schedule as your labs, so every result is reviewed with you.</li><li><strong>Shipping</strong> for the full year.</li></ul><p>Other forms of testosterone such as creams, peptides and other medications cost extra, and the price stays the same after year one. Another may offer a broader evaluation of your hormones, thyroid, metabolism, sleep, and other factors contributing to how you feel.</p><p>What happens three months into treatment when your testosterone levels have improved, but you're still exhausted, gaining weight, and struggling with brain fog? Does your clinic investigate further, or simply adjust your dose? And when your lab results don't fit the expected pattern, or another medical condition complicates your treatment, does your care team have the expertise and resources to look beyond testosterone?</p><p>Questions worth asking any clinic before you pay:</p><ul><li>What's included in the monthly price, and what's billed separately?</li><li>Do you confirm low testosterone with a repeat morning test, and check LH, FSH and SHBG to find out <em>why</em> it's low?</li><li>Will you look at sleep, weight and metabolic health before prescribing?</li><li>How often will I have labs and see a provider in the first year?</li><li>What's my total cost for the first 12 months?</li><li>How do you handle fertility if I want children in the future?</li></ul><h2>FAQs</h2><h3>How much is TRT per month?</h3><p>Advertised prices start from about $30 a month and vary widely depending on what's included. Generic injections cost the least, while brand-name gels and oral capsules can run $400 to $2,000 a month for the medication alone. Compare first-year totals, because labs and visits are often billed separately.</p><h3>How much is TRT without insurance?</h3><p>At RHM, plans start from $299 a month. Other forms of testosterone, peptides and other medications cost extra.</p><h3>Is TRT covered by insurance?</h3><p>Sometimes. Insurers usually require documented low testosterone on repeat testing plus symptoms, often with prior authorization, and may cover only certain forms.</p><h3>Can I use my HSA or FSA for TRT?</h3><p>Often, yes. Prescribed medicines and medical care generally qualify as medical expenses under IRS rules. Check your plan's terms.</p><h3>Can I raise testosterone without TRT?</h3><p>Sometimes, depending on the cause.
