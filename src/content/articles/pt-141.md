<p>Desire used to show up on its own. Now it doesn't, or it shows up and your body doesn't follow. You love your partner, you're not unhappy, and you can't explain why the interest faded. Maybe you've been told it's stress, age or "just a phase," and now you're reading about a peptide called PT-141.</p><p>The change felt real. The explanation never fully did.</p><p>And it reaches far past the bedroom. You start going to bed at different times. You pull back from a hug because you're unsure where it leads. You turn your partner down again and feel guilty about it for days. They start to wonder if you're still attracted to them, and you can't find the words to explain that you are.</p><p>PT-141, also called bremelanotide, is a peptide that acts on melanocortin receptors in the brain to increase sexual desire and arousal. It's FDA-approved as Vyleesi, an injection for premenopausal women with low sexual desire that causes distress. In men it's used off-label, most often when blood-flow medications haven't worked.</p><h2>What Low Desire Feels Like From the Inside</h2><p>Low desire is hard to describe, even to the person closest to you. Many of the people we see have carried thoughts like these for months before saying them out loud:</p><ul><li>"I love my partner, but they think I'm not attracted to them anymore."</li><li>"I feel guilty every time I say no."</li><li>"I used to feel passionate and confident. I don't recognize myself."</li><li>"I want to want it. The desire just isn't there."</li><li>"Is this what the rest of my life is going to look like?"</li></ul><p>If any of those sound familiar, you're far from alone, and there's nothing wrong with you for feeling them. Desire is tied to connection, confidence and how you see yourself, so losing it can feel like losing a part of who you are. That deserves a physician's full attention. At RHM, care starts there: with what has changed in your life and your relationship, before any talk of treatment.</p><h2>When Low Desire Gets Treated as a Side Note</h2><p>Low desire rarely gets its own appointment. It gets folded into whatever else you mention:</p><ul><li>Low libido gets "it's stress."</li><li>Erection problems get a pill.</li><li>Fatigue gets more coffee.</li><li>Weight gain gets "eat less."</li><li>Poor sleep gets melatonin.</li><li>Feeling flat gets an antidepressant, which can lower desire further.</li></ul><p>What if they're connected?</p><p>Desire depends on hormones, blood flow, energy, sleep and mood all working at once. This usually comes down to how care is set up. A short visit is built to rule out disease, so each symptom gets its own fix and nobody looks at how they fit together.</p><h2>We're Not Here to Convince You That You Need PT-141</h2><p><strong>We're not here to convince you that you need PT-141. We're here to help you understand why your desire changed.</strong></p><p>Sometimes PT-141 is a good fit. Often the change in desire is coming from somewhere else:</p><ul><li>Sex hormones, including testosterone, estrogen and progesterone</li><li>Thyroid function</li><li>Insulin and metabolic health</li><li>Inflammation</li><li>Sleep and stress</li><li>Nutrient status, such as iron and vitamin D</li></ul><p>"Your labs look normal."</p><p>Okay, but which labs?</p><ul><li>A routine panel often skips testosterone, estradiol and prolactin, the hormones most closely tied to desire.</li><li>Reference ranges are population averages, so a result can sit in range and still be low for you.</li></ul><p>At RHM we read more than 160 biomarkers across those systems as one picture before choosing a treatment. For women, that work happens through our <a href="/women/sexual-health">sexual health care</a>. For men, it happens through our <a href="/men/erectile-dysfunction-clinic">erectile dysfunction and libido clinic</a>.</p><p><strong>If you're ready to understand what's behind your changes in desire,</strong> a VIP call is your first step toward becoming an RHM patient. We'll talk through what's changed, how it's affecting you and your relationship and what you want back, then map out the evaluation that finds what's driving it. <a href="/scheduling">Book a VIP call</a>.</p><h2>Desire Runs on a Circuit, and PT-141 Works on One Part of It</h2><p><em>PT-141 turns up the desire signal in the brain. How well that works depends on the rest of the circuit: your hormones, your blood flow and how much energy you have left at the end of the day.</em></p><p>You may have been told low libido is "all in your head" or all about hormones. Thinking of desire as a circuit explains why both are partly true.</p><h3>1. The brain signal</h3><p>Desire starts in the brain. PT-141 is a synthetic version of a natural hormone called alpha-melanocyte-stimulating hormone, and it binds strongly to the melanocortin-4 receptor, which is involved in sexual response (<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31429064/" target="_blank">Dhillon and Keam, Drugs, 2019</a>). This is the part PT-141 works on.</p><h3>2. The hormones that prime it</h3><p>Testosterone, estrogen and progesterone set how responsive that circuit is. Thyroid hormone and prolactin affect it too. When these shift, as they do in perimenopause or with falling testosterone, the signal has less to work with.</p><h3>3. Blood flow and tissue</h3><p>Arousal needs healthy blood vessels and responsive tissue. Insulin resistance, high blood pressure and low estrogen in vaginal tissue can all get in the way, even when desire is there.</p><h3>4. Energy, sleep and mood</h3><p>Exhaustion, poor sleep, stress, depression and some medications can turn desire down at the source. No peptide fully overrides a body that's running on empty.</p><p>Each part belongs to a different system. That's why we test before we prescribe.</p><p>When it feels like your body has stopped cooperating, knowing which part changed is a relief in itself. Mapping that circuit with you is the first thing our physicians do, so the plan fits your body, your health and your relationship.</p><h2>What the PT-141 Trials Actually Show</h2><p>You may have seen PT-141 described as a "female Viagra." The trial data tells a more useful story.</p><p>The two Phase 3 RECONNECT trials enrolled 1,267 premenopausal women with low sexual desire, average age 39, for 24 weeks (<a href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6819021/" target="_blank">Kingsberg et al., Obstetrics & Gynecology, 2019</a>):</p><ul><li><strong>Desire improved</strong> more than with placebo, by 0.30 and 0.42 points on the desire scale used in the trials.</li><li><strong>Distress about low desire</strong> went down more than with placebo.</li><li><strong>Nausea was common:</strong> 40% of women on bremelanotide had it, compared with 1.3% on placebo. Most cases were mild to moderate, and 8.1% stopped treatment because of it.</li></ul><p>The improvement was measurable but modest. For some women it makes a meaningful difference. For others, the cause of low desire sits elsewhere in the circuit, and that's where treatment does more good.</p><p>That's why we start with the cause. When the average effect is modest, the person matters more than the peptide: your hormones, your sleep, your medications and your relationship all shape whether PT-141 helps you. Finding which part of the circuit has changed is the work RHM's physicians do with you.</p><figure class="w-richtext-figure-type-image w-richtext-align-fullwidth" style="max-width:1536px" data-rt-type="image" data-rt-align="fullwidth" data-rt-max-width="1536px"><div><img alt="Smiling woman in her forties resting her chin on her hand while talking with her partner on a sofa at home" src="/images/cms/6af1b64b8ca6c6147281bcd6_6af1b5f1fc15c54118af056d_couple-reconnecting-pt141-results.jpeg" loading="lazy"></div></figure><h2>PT-141 for Women</h2><p>You may have been told that low desire after kids, during perimenopause or in a long relationship is just how it goes. Sometimes it's more than that, and it's treatable.</p><p>Vyleesi is approved for premenopausal women with acquired, generalized low desire that causes distress. The FDA label states it isn't approved for postmenopausal women or for men, and it isn't meant to enhance sexual performance (<a href="https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/lookup.cfm?setid=f1d0c1b5-2f39-4bad-a6a4-0066e3ad5dcf" target="_blank">Vyleesi prescribing information, DailyMed</a>).</p><p>It isn't the only option. Flibanserin (Addyi) is a daily pill approved for the same group of premenopausal women (<a href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5298357/" target="_blank">Gelman and Atrio, Therapeutic Advances in Chronic Disease, 2017</a>). After menopause, the strongest evidence points to a different treatment:</p><ul><li>An international consensus statement found that testosterone therapy is supported for postmenopausal women diagnosed with low sexual desire.</li><li>It recommends that diagnosis come after a full assessment of physical, psychological and relationship factors (<a href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6821450/" target="_blank">Davis et al., Journal of Clinical Endocrinology & Metabolism, 2019</a>).</li></ul><p>If your desire changed alongside hot flashes, broken sleep or irregular cycles, our guide to the <a href="/articles/signs-you-need-hrt">signs you need hormone replacement therapy</a> may explain more of the picture.</p><p>Which of these fits depends on your stage of life, your health history and what's driving the change. Working that out with you, in a conversation where nothing is off limits, is what our physicians are here for.</p><h2>PT-141 for Men</h2><p>You may have been offered a stronger dose of the same pill when the first one stopped working. PT-141 works through a different pathway, which is why it gets attention in men's health.</p><p>Erection pills such as sildenafil work on blood flow. PT-141 works on the brain's desire and arousal signals. In a randomized trial of 342 men whose erectile dysfunction hadn't responded to sildenafil, 33.5% had a positive response with bremelanotide, compared with 8.5% on placebo (<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18206919/" target="_blank">Safarinejad and Hosseini, Journal of Urology, 2008</a>).</p><p>That's encouraging, but it's one trial, and PT-141 isn't FDA-approved for men. Low testosterone is a common reason for low desire in men, and the cause decides the treatment:</p><ul><li>If testosterone is low, treating it may bring desire back on its own. Our <a href="/men/hormone-therapy">hormone therapy for men</a> starts with that question.</li><li>If blood flow is the issue, metabolic health and vascular care come first.</li><li>If desire and arousal signals are the issue, PT-141 may help, alone or alongside other treatment.</li></ul><p>If you've started to feel like you're letting your partner down, a stronger pill can seem like the only option left. A physician who looks at your hormones, blood flow and desire together can show you which one has changed, and that's where your plan at RHM begins.</p><h2>How PT-141 Is Used</h2><p>You may have pictured a daily shot or a pill you take every morning. PT-141 works differently, and knowing how it's used helps set realistic expectations.</p><ul><li><strong>As needed, not daily.</strong> The approved form is a small autoinjector used under the skin of the abdomen or thigh, at least 45 minutes before sex.</li><li><strong>With limits on frequency.</strong> The label limits how often it can be used, because doses close together add to the blood pressure effect and raise the chance of skin darkening.</li><li><strong>With a check-in point.</strong> The label advises stopping after 8 weeks if there's no improvement, so it's easy to tell whether it's working for you.</li></ul><p>Your physician decides the form, the amount and how often it fits your health, so we don't list doses here.</p><h2>PT-141 Side Effects and Who Should Avoid It</h2><p>You may have read that peptides are "natural," so they're safe. PT-141 is well studied, and like any medication it has known effects:</p><ul><li><strong>Nausea</strong> is the most common side effect, especially with the first doses.</li><li><strong>Flushing and headache</strong> were the other common effects in trials.</li><li><strong>Blood pressure</strong> rises temporarily after a dose. Vyleesi is contraindicated in people with uncontrolled high blood pressure or known heart disease.</li><li><strong>Skin darkening</strong> in patches on the face, gums or breasts was reported in about 1% of trial patients.</li><li><strong>Naltrexone:</strong> PT-141 can lower the effect of oral naltrexone, so it shouldn't be combined with naltrexone used to treat alcohol or opioid addiction.</li></ul><p>These come from the FDA label. They're the reason a physician should check your blood pressure, heart health and medications before you start.</p><h2>Prescribed PT-141 and "Research" Peptides</h2><p>You can find PT-141 nasal sprays and vials sold online as "research peptides." They're cheap and easy to order, so it's worth asking a few questions first:</p><ul><li>Who is prescribing it, and did anyone check your blood pressure and heart health?</li><li>Where is it coming from, and what quality standards apply to what's in the vial?</li><li>Who is watching how your body responds, and what happens if it doesn't help?</li><li>Did anyone ask why your desire changed in the first place?</li></ul><p>The FDA-approved form is an injection prescribed by a clinician. Products labeled "not for human use" aren't made or tested for people.</p><p>Ordering a vial means handling all of this on your own.
