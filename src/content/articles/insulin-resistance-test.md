<h2>When "normal" doesn't feel like an answer</h2><p>Cravings that don't track with what you ate. Weight that won't move the way it used to, even with the same effort. Energy crashes mid-afternoon, or a family history of diabetes that makes you want a real answer instead of a guess. Maybe you've had blood work before and it came back "normal," and the visit moved on before the question felt settled. Or maybe insulin resistance was never actually part of what got tested — most standard panels don't include it.</p><p>Wanting to know, specifically, isn't overreacting. Normal is an average. You are not an average, and a glucose or A1c number sitting inside a typical range doesn't rule out insulin resistance on its own.</p><h2>Why "just check my blood sugar" isn't the same question</h2><p>Glucose and A1c measure what's already happened — glucose reflects your blood sugar right now, and A1c reflects an average over the past few months. Insulin resistance is what's driving toward that outcome, often years earlier. Your pancreas can compensate for a while by producing more insulin to keep glucose in range, which is exactly why glucose and A1c can still look fine while insulin resistance is already underway. An insulin resistance test is aimed at catching that earlier stage, not the point where glucose has already started to climb.</p><h2>What an insulin resistance test actually measures</h2><p>The core of it is two numbers, read together. Fasting insulin shows how much insulin your pancreas is putting out after an overnight fast — if it's elevated while glucose is still normal, that's the pattern of early insulin resistance: the pancreas working harder to keep blood sugar in range. Fasting glucose shows where blood sugar sits on its own. Combined, the two feed a calculation called HOMA-IR, which gives a single number that reflects how well insulin is doing its job rather than looking at either value in isolation.</p><p>A1c adds a longer-term view — it reflects average blood sugar over roughly the past three months, which is useful context but, on its own, moves later than insulin resistance does. Some evaluations also look at a two-hour glucose response after a sugary drink (an oral glucose tolerance test), though it's used less often outside of a specific diagnostic question. Worth knowing going in: standard clinical care doesn't always test for insulin resistance directly — glucose and A1c are what most routine panels default to, with fasting insulin and HOMA-IR ordered less consistently unless someone specifically asks for them (<a href="https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/diabetes/overview/what-is-diabetes/prediabetes-insulin-resistance" target="_blank">NIDDK</a>).</p><figure class="w-richtext-figure-type-image w-richtext-align-fullwidth" style="max-width:1448px" data-rt-type="image" data-rt-align="fullwidth" data-rt-max-width="1448px"><div><img alt="Woman preparing healthy salad bowl in kitchen" src="/images/cms/6a79f459ce29b72b51f853b9_healthy_food.png" loading="lazy"></div></figure><h2>Is there an at-home insulin resistance test?</h2><p>Yes, with a real caveat about what "at home" actually covers. A mailed collection kit can capture fasting insulin, fasting glucose, and A1c from a small blood sample, the same markers a clinical draw would collect — the difference is where the sample is taken, not what's being measured. That makes at-home collection a genuinely useful way to remove the friction of a lab visit, especially for something like fasting insulin that isn't part of a typical annual panel.</p><p>The limitation shows up with consumer kits built around a single marker. An at-home A1c-only kit, for example, can tell you your three-month blood sugar average, but it can't tell you about insulin resistance specifically — A1c is a late-moving marker, so a normal A1c result from a single-marker kit doesn't mean insulin resistance isn't present. At-home testing is a legitimate input, not a full evaluation on its own, and it works best as one part of a broader read rather than a standalone answer.</p><h2>What the numbers can mean</h2><p>A HOMA-IR or fasting insulin result outside the typical reference range is useful information — it's a reason to have a specific conversation with a physician, not a number that diagnoses you on its own. Reference ranges vary somewhat by lab, and where a number falls matters less in isolation than it does next to your symptoms, history, and other lab markers. Two people with a similar HOMA-IR can be dealing with different underlying pictures.</p><p>This is also where "in range" and "optimal" can diverge. A result that technically falls inside a lab's reference range isn't automatically the best read on your metabolic health — ranges are built from population averages, and a number that's technically normal can still be higher than it was for you five years ago, or higher than it needs to be. That's a distinction worth having a physician walk through with you, not one to resolve by comparing your number to a general cutoff online.</p><p><strong><em>Dr. Rand Insight:</em></strong></p><div data-rt-embed-type='true'><blockquote class="blockquote"><p><em>“A single insulin resistance test tells you where one number sits today. It doesn't tell you the whole story on its own — that comes from reading it next to everything else going on in your body.”</em></p><div class="blockquote_profile"><img src="/images/cms/6a362af7235f0819a188a49f_dr_rand.jpg" loading="lazy" alt="Profile Picture of Dr. Rand" class="profile_img"><p class="body-m fw-500 color-dark">Dr. Rand McClain</p></div></blockquote></div><h2>What RHM evaluates</h2><p>Our approach starts with fasting insulin, fasting glucose, and HOMA-IR as part of a broader <a href="/women/thyroid">160+ biomarker evaluation</a>, collected through an at-home kit and read by a physician alongside thyroid function, cortisol, sex hormones, and body composition — because the same insulin resistance number can point in different directions depending on what else is happening. For women, insulin resistance is a well-documented driver of <a href="/women/pcos">PCOS</a>, often showing up alongside irregular cycles, androgen changes, or <a href="/articles/pcos-weight-loss">weight that doesn't respond the way it used to</a>. For men, it frequently intersects with testosterone levels, which is part of why a single isolated lab value rarely tells the full story either way. A physician reviewing the full picture is what turns a HOMA-IR number into something you can actually act on.</p><h2>What this can look like next</h2><p>A clearer starting point, instead of a "normal" result that didn't fully answer the question. For a lot of people, it's less about a single dramatic diagnosis and more about finally having real numbers behind cravings, energy, or <a href="/women/weight-loss-resistance">weight that resists the usual levers</a> — something to bring into a conversation with a physician rather than carry around as a guess.</p><p>Results vary, and a lab value is one input among several. It doesn't replace an evaluation, and it isn't a diagnosis on its own — it's the starting point for one.</p><h2>Why RHM</h2><p>We're physician-led, and the read starts with your actual numbers next to your symptoms and history, not a single cutoff applied the same way to everyone. That's the difference between a HOMA-IR result sitting alone on a lab report and the same number interpreted inside a full metabolic and hormonal picture.</p><h2>FAQs</h2><h3>How do you test for insulin resistance?</h3><p>Most commonly through fasting insulin and fasting glucose, combined into a calculation called HOMA-IR; A1c is often included as a longer-term trend marker. Standard checkups don't always test insulin directly, so it's worth asking for specifically if it isn't part of your usual panel.</p><h3>Is there an at-home insulin resistance test?</h3><p>Yes — a mailed collection kit can capture fasting insulin, glucose, and A1c from a small sample, the same core markers a clinical draw would measure. Single-marker consumer kits (an A1c-only kit, for example) are more limited, since A1c moves later than insulin resistance does.</p><h3>What blood test shows insulin resistance?</h3><p>Fasting insulin and fasting glucose, read together as HOMA-IR, are the main markers. A1c and, less commonly, an oral glucose tolerance test can add context, though they tend to shift only after insulin resistance has already been present for a while.</p><h3>What is a normal insulin resistance test range?</h3><p>Reference ranges vary by lab, and a number's meaning depends on the person, not just the cutoff. A result outside the typical range is a reason to talk with a physician about what it means for you specifically, not a self-diagnosis.</p><h3>Is there a specific blood test for insulin resistance, or is it part of a regular checkup?</h3><p>It's usually not part of a routine annual panel by default. Most standard checkups default to glucose and A1c; fasting insulin and HOMA-IR are typically ordered when someone specifically asks or a physician has a reason to look further.</p><h3>Can insulin resistance be tested for without a fasting blood draw?</h3><p>Fasting is standard for the core markers (insulin and glucose), since eating beforehand affects both values. A1c doesn't require fasting, which is part of why it's more commonly included in routine panels even though it's a later-moving marker.</p><h3>What's included in an insulin resistance test panel?</h3><p>At minimum, fasting insulin and fasting glucose to calculate HOMA-IR, often alongside A1c. A more complete evaluation, like RHM's, reads those alongside thyroid, cortisol, sex hormones, and body composition rather than reporting the insulin markers in isolation.</p><h3>How is insulin resistance testing different from a standard glucose or A1c test?</h3><p>Glucose and A1c reflect blood sugar after it's already shifted. Fasting insulin and HOMA-IR are aimed at the stage before that — when the pancreas is compensating by producing more insulin, which is why insulin resistance can be present for years with glucose and A1c still reading normal.</p><h2>Ready to know what your numbers actually mean?</h2><p><a href="/scheduling">Book a VIP call</a> with our team.
